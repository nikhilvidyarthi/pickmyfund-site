Market-neutral, explained simply

Most investing works only if the market goes up. A market-neutral strategy tries to make the market’s direction matter much less.

3 min readPickMyFund

Directional versus neutral

When you buy a stock or an equity fund, you are making a directional bet: if the market rises, you gain; if it falls, you lose. That is perfectly sensible for long-term money, but it means your result depends heavily on which way the market moves.

A market-neutral position is built so that a rise or a fall in the market, on its own, has a smaller effect. The result comes from something else instead.

An everyday comparison

Think of a trader in a wholesale market who buys a crate in the morning and sells it the same afternoon. Whether prices are high or low that season matters less to them. What matters is the margin between buying and selling, and whether they can repeat it reliably.

Market-neutral strategies look for that kind of margin in financial markets. Common sources include:

  • Time decay in options, where option prices lose value as expiry approaches.
  • The gap between two related prices, such as a futures contract and the same stock in the cash market.
  • Pairs of positions that offset each other, so that a move in one is largely cancelled by the other.

Arbitrage plus algo: a second income from the same money

Many businesses leave surplus cash in a current account or a liquid fund. We set it up in two layers instead.

  • Layer 1: an arbitrage fund. The money goes into an arbitrage fund, which buys shares in the cash market and sells the same shares in futures, locking in the small gap between the two prices. Because the two sides offset each other, the return depends little on whether the market rises or falls.
  • Layer 2: the Algo Desk. Units of the arbitrage fund can be pledged with the broker as margin, after a haircut. That margin backs hedged algo strategies, which aim to add a second stream of income on top of the arbitrage return.

The same capital keeps earning its arbitrage return while it also supports the algo strategies. The second layer is where the risk sits: algo income varies from month to month and can be negative, and if losses run beyond the cash available, pledged units can be sold to cover them.

Neutral is not risk-free

A hedge reduces the effect of the market’s direction; it does not remove risk. Sudden gaps at the open, sharp jumps in volatility, thin trading and hedges that do not offset perfectly can all cause losses, sometimes large ones. Derivatives are leveraged, so gains and losses can both be bigger than the money put up.

SEBI’s own study of the futures and options market found that about nine in ten individual traders lost money. That is why we build every strategy around risk limits first, and why this is not suitable for everyone.
