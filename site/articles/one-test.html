Why we run one test, every Friday

Most portfolios are built from a pile of inputs: a tip, a headline, a hunch. Ours is built from one question, asked of every stock, the same way, every week.

3 min readPickMyFund

The problem with many reasons

Ask why a stock is in most portfolios and you will hear a different reason for each one. A friend recommended it. It was in the news. It "looked cheap". It had a good story.

Each reason may be fine on its own. Together they make it impossible to tell what is working. When every decision is made a different way, you cannot learn from the wins, and the losses are always explained away.

One question, asked of everyone

Top 20 starts with about 730 NSE-listed stocks. Every Friday after the close, each of them is put through the same test of price strength, and the whole list is ranked from strongest to weakest. The strongest make the cut. Those that weaken over time make way for those that strengthen.

There is no shortlist of favourites, no override for a good story and no exception for a stock we like. A name we have never heard of gets exactly the same treatment as a household brand.

Why one test works for us

  • It is consistent. The same question every week means every holding is there for the same reason.
  • It takes us out of it. Rules do not get attached to a stock, and they do not panic on a bad day.
  • It lets leaders run. A stock stays while it keeps passing the test, so winners are not sold just because they have gone up.
  • It is checkable. Every change is recorded in a weekly note, so you can see exactly what moved and when.

Why Friday, and not every day

Daily prices are noisy. A weekly reading smooths out much of that noise, and it means changes happen at one calm, planned moment instead of in reaction to every headline. Fewer, deliberate changes also keep costs down.

What one test does not do

It does not predict the future. Strength can fade, and markets can turn quickly. There will be weeks and months when a rules-based book trails the index, and stretches when it falls with everything else. A concentrated book of twenty stocks can also swing more than a broad index fund.

We accept those costs in exchange for something most portfolios lack: a clear, repeatable reason for every decision.
